# Interpreting simulations

Updated 2026-10-30. Applies to product descriptions, pitches, videos and report interpretation. This document clarifies terminology; it does not change the app's stored fields or current trust-label names.

**Simulated findings are hypotheses, not evidence about real users.** Gnomi helps teams choose what to investigate. It does not establish user needs, validate a design, or predict adoption, conversion, sentiment or the prevalence of a problem in a real population.

## What is generated, and what is recorded

Personas are fictional profiles assembled from team-approved audience assumptions, seeded trait sampling and model-written candidates. See [Persona generation](persona-generation.md#how-simulated-users-are-made) for the complete process. Configuring a language or access need does not establish that an agent behaves like a person with that experience.

| Output | What it tells you | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Browser action, screenshot or error log | What the software and agent did in this environment during this run | That people would take the same actions or experience the same difficulty |
| Persona quote, think-aloud note or opinion | Text generated by the model under that persona's instructions | A participant's testimony, internal reasoning, emotion or preference |
| An answer from a question after the run | A new generation from that persona's log, for exploring what the log shows | A memory of why the persona acted, or a reason anyone held |
| Ranked friction theme or suggested change | A candidate problem or intervention worth investigating | A validated user problem or an effective solution |
| Segment counts, sentiment and social spread | Outcomes within the configured simulation | Market proportions, real-world incidence, independent human agreement or word-of-mouth reach |
| What-if differences between matched runs | How one post the team added moved numbers inside the simulator | That the post would do anything among real people, or by how much |

Existing report fields named `evidence` contain **simulation records**. They make a run inspectable. A reproducible browser error can establish a technical defect under the tested conditions, but its impact on people still needs investigation. Several agents repeating a complaint is not independent corroboration: they can share model biases, and social rounds deliberately expose them to one another.

A segment can be grounded in a team's analytics or research notes, and the report's methods then name those sources. Grounded personas are still fictional, and their findings are still hypotheses: real data can make a persona look more credible without making it more accurate. Data used to write personas cannot also test them.

**Returning personas** remember an earlier run of theirs, so such a report says little about first use, and it says so at the top. Their memory is a short summary handed to a model, not what a person who came back would recall, and nothing yet shows that they behave like returning people. A persona that repeats a complaint from its earlier visit counts apart from those who met it first-hand: "2 more reported it after remembering it from an earlier visit". A repeat can still be a real problem that is still there, so read it as a place to look. A returning side and a first-use side are not like for like, so a comparison says so and the CI gate cannot decide. Findings from returning personas are hypotheses like any other. A brief rerun, where one persona follows the team's own words, says so at the top too, and a comparison or the gate treats it the same way.

## Read trust labels within the simulation

- **Confirmed:** a generated self-report aligns with an observed signal under the report's matching rules. This means corroboration within the run, not confirmation by real participants.
- **Observed:** an instrumented signal was recorded. It can still be benign or caused by the agent.
- **Reported:** a persona generated a complaint without a matching instrumented signal.

Likewise, `strong`, `some` and `single` describe the report's internal support counts. Scores and intervals used for ranking do not supply calibrated confidence about real users. Repeating a result with another seed or model can check robustness within the simulation; it does not turn the result into human evidence. `Fix now`, `Next` and `Later` suggest where to look first within one run, from its severity and support counts; they do not measure impact on real people.

## Findings by screen

The findings list has two views. **By priority** is the default: the ranked list. **By screen** groups the same findings by the screen they happened on. It is in the app only; exported HTML, PDF and Markdown files have a **Findings by screen** section instead, with each screen's picture once.

- A **screen** is a page pattern, shown as its path (`/jobs/:id`), or a design frame, shown by its name. A finding can sit on several screens if it was met on several pages.
- Each card leads with its top finding's picture: the first picture on that screen, in the order the findings are listed (by section, then rank).
- A **pin** marks an element on the picture, numbered to match its findings. A pin appears only when a finding's own screenshot is the card's picture and the element's position on it is known. A finding whose own screenshot is a different one shows it as a thumbnail instead. Findings with the same element share one pin.
- A picture comes from a persona without simulated vision or a screen reader when one met the screen. Only when none did is it that persona's screenshot, which may show emulated colour-blind or blurred vision.
- **Across many screens** holds a finding met on 4 or more pages. It is one group, not a copy on each screen. **Not tied to one screen** holds findings with no page, mostly survey answers.
- Screens are ordered by how many personas reached them.
- Runs recorded before steps stored their viewport have pictures but no pins.

The view moves no rank, count, trust label or priority. It only regroups the findings that are already there.

## From a hypothesis to a study

1. Inspect the linked steps and screenshots. Separate an application failure from a model mistake, an incomplete environment or an invented complaint.
2. State the candidate problem and the uncertainty: “Hiring managers may struggle to choose a person when the field requires an exact username.” Keep the simulated count and run context attached.
3. Test the same task with relevant real participants without feeding them the generated complaint. Record what they actually do and say. Use appropriate product data when investigating frequency or impact.
4. Record whether the human observations support, contradict or leave the hypothesis unresolved. Evaluate a proposed change separately; a plausible suggestion is not a proven fix.

Keep human observations and simulation outputs separately attributed in reports and design reviews. A lack of simulated friction is not evidence that a flow works for people.

**A study plan** (`gnomi study`, or Plan a study in the report) is generated from the top friction findings of the team's own product. It leaves out objections, competitors, findings the evidence check set aside and findings only the accessibility scan made, because a scan finding needs an audit, and it says so. Participants see only the tasks and the screener. Code drops any entry that repeats four or more consecutive words of a finding, its description or its evidence, and the plan says how many it dropped, but a paraphrase can still lead a participant, so read the plan before you use it. The file opens by saying the hypotheses are simulated and unproven and that a model wrote the plan: keep the hypotheses from participants.

**A study outcome** (`gnomi outcome`, or the control on a finding) records step 4 in your team's name, as "Your team's study, 12 Oct 2026: supported". It is what people did, so it stays apart from the simulation: it changes no simulated count, rank, priority, trust level or written summary, and it reaches no model. The latest for a finding counts, so correct one by recording another. Keep names and contact details out of a note: it stays in the project's log, even after a correction, and appears in reports and exports. An outcome belongs to its run: a repeat set or a comparison does not carry it across, and a finding that comes back in another run needs its own study. The count over the top five findings is a tally of what your team studied, not a measure of Gnomi's accuracy.

**Editing before sharing** (`gnomi edit`, or the controls on a finding) changes exports only. A hidden finding stays on screen, marked, and leaves every export. A reworded finding shows the original beside the new words, and an export marks it as reworded by the team. Only a title and a description can change, never a number from the run. Keep names and contact details out of a rewording: it stays in the project's log, even after a reset, and appears in exports. Every copy the team changed says how many findings were hidden and reworded, and nothing removes that line, because a copy that hides what it left out would mislead its reader. So read a shared copy as a team's selection from a run, and leaving a finding out is no evidence against it. Its written summary says what leads "this copy", not the run, once a finding is hidden. No model, repeat set, comparison or gate reads an edit.

**A question after the run** (`gnomi ask`, or a chat in the app: the inspector's, or Ask the personas on the report page) gets a new answer from the model, written from that persona's log. It is not a memory of why the persona acted: the persona had no reasons to remember, and a plausible answer can be pure invention even when the log says nothing. The prompt asks the model to say it does not know when the log does not show a reason, but it may not. So use an answer to explore where to look in the steps and screenshots, never as evidence for a finding. `gnomi ask` labels every answer "Generated after the run. This is a new answer from the model, not a memory of why this persona acted." In the app every chat opens with that label, and each answer in it is marked "Generated after the run". Answers stay in the CLI's output and those chats, out of findings, counts, the written summary, study plans and every report file and export, as a reader of a file could take one for a finding. A question gets the earlier questions to that persona in that run and their answers, so a follow-up such as "why?" knows what it follows. Those answers are generations too, not memories: the prompt keeps them apart from the log and tells the model not to repeat a reason from them as true unless the log shows it, but it may, so a reason that comes back in a later answer is no better evidence than the first time. A question costs money, and it makes no finding more correct. Keep names and contact details out of a question: it goes to the model and stays in the project's log.

## What-if results

A what-if (`gnomi run --what-if`, or What-if post before a run) compares matched runs with and without one post your team wrote, and it is the easiest result here to over-read. Spread is relative and uncalibrated, so the differences say what the simulator did with the post, and a difference inside the simulator does not establish a real-world effect ([new_simulation.md §4](new_simulation.md#4-population-and-behavioral-model)). The personas read the post as one persona's, picked by the seed, but it is your team's text, and the report says so. It is never that persona's report, so it stays out of first-hand counts, themes and trust.

Report the mean difference with its interval, or with the note that says why there is none, and with the number of pairs. Do not turn a difference into a percentage, a forecast or a cause: say "moved inside the simulator", never "will increase", "caused" or "lift". Fewer than three pairs give no interval, and an interval that excludes zero still describes the simulator. Test a post that matters with real people.

## Wording to reuse

> Gnomi samples traits from the audience settings you define, then uses a language model to write fictional profiles for review. These personas encode assumptions about an audience; they are not a representative sample of real users.

> Simulated findings are hypotheses, not evidence about real users. Screenshots and logs show what happened in the simulation; quotes and opinions are generated. Use the report to choose what to investigate with real people.

> Inside the simulator, adding this post moved these numbers. That does not establish a real-world effect. Spread is relative and uncalibrated.
